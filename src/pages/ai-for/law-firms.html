That call is where a small firm wins or loses a matter before any legal work happens. It is a front desk tool. It does not draft, research or advise.

Who calls a law firm, and when

Calls to a firm follow the event that caused them, not your office hours. The pattern changes by practice area, so the intake script does too.

What the AI intake agent collects on the call

Order matters on a legal intake call. The agent is configured to gather the minimum the firm needs to decide whether to talk to this person, in this sequence:

  1. Who is calling and how to reach them. Name, the best number and email, and whether it is safe to leave a message there. In family and criminal matters that last question matters.
  2. The names for your conflict check. The caller, the other party, and anyone else you list on your intake form. These go to the top of the summary so a person at the firm can run the check before anyone hears the story.
  3. A short description, not the whole story. Practice area, county, a sentence or two about what happened. The agent is told not to draw out detail the firm has not decided it wants yet.
  4. Deadlines the caller already knows about. A court date, a letter with a response date, a hearing. Anything time sensitive is flagged at the top of the email.
  5. The consultation. Booked into the calendar you give us, under the rules you set: which attorney, what length, whether it is by phone or in person.

The ethics lines we build into a law firm intake agent

We are not lawyers and this is not legal advice. These are the published ABA sources a firm should read before it puts any AI on its phone, and how each one shapes what we configure. States adopt the Model Rules with their own changes, so check your state's version with your own ethics counsel.

ABA Formal Opinion 512

Issued July 29, 2024, it was the ABA's first formal ethics opinion on lawyers using generative AI. It covers competence, confidentiality, communication with clients, supervision and fees. In plain terms: know what the tool does, protect client information when it goes into one, and do not treat a boilerplate line in an engagement letter as consent. We give you a written description of what the agent does with call information so you can make that judgment.

Model Rule 5.3: supervision

Lawyers with managerial authority must make reasonable efforts to have measures in place giving reasonable assurance that nonlawyer assistance fits the lawyer's own obligations, and Opinion 512 applies that thinking to AI tools. Practically, a lawyer at the firm should approve the intake script, listen to test calls and read the summaries. We do not switch the agent on until someone at the firm has signed off.

Model Rule 1.18: prospective clients

Someone who consults a lawyer about possibly hiring them is owed confidentiality even if they never become a client, and what they say can create a conflict. That is why the script asks for conflict names first and keeps the description short: the rule's screening exception looks at whether the lawyer avoided learning more than was reasonably necessary.

Model Rule 1.6 and Rule 7.3

Rule 1.6(c) asks for reasonable efforts to prevent unauthorized disclosure of client information, so access to call recordings and summaries is limited to people you name. Rule 7.3 restricts live person-to-person solicitation for pecuniary gain, so the agent answers people who called you and calls back only people who asked to be called.

AI for small law firms and solo practices

A large firm has a receptionist and an intake department. A solo practitioner has a cell phone that rings during a hearing. For small firms the value of AI is less about cleverness and more about coverage: someone answers, in the firm's name, while the lawyer is in court, with a client, driving between courthouses or asleep.

What a small firm usually asks us for:

  • A greeting that sounds like the firm, not like a call center, with the attorney's name if you want it said
  • An honest answer to "is this a real person?" (it says it is an AI assistant for the firm)
  • Booking only into slots you have opened, so a court day stays blocked
  • A transfer to your cell for a short list of situations you define, with everything else held for the morning
  • Answers to the questions you hear every week: where you are, whether you charge for a first consult, what to bring, whether you handle a given type of case

AI for personal injury law firms

Personal injury calls have their own rhythm. The caller may be at home after an emergency room visit, may be calling for someone who is still in the hospital, and may have just had a first conversation with an insurance adjuster. They often call several firms. An AI answering service for law firms helps here because it can take that call at nine on a Saturday night with a calm, consistent set of questions.

What we configure for injury practices in particular:

Incident basics

Date, location and type of incident, other people or companies involved, and whether a police or incident report exists. No questions about fault, and no opinion on the case.

Treatment, in one line

Only whether the caller was treated and where, so the attorney can decide how urgent the follow-up is. The agent does not ask for medical details or records.

Deadline flag

Limitation periods vary by state and by claim. The agent does not state one. It flags every injury call as time sensitive for an attorney to review.

Some injury firms ask about outbound AI calling to buy leads or reach accident victims. We do not set that up. Beyond the solicitation questions Rule 7.3 raises, the FCC's February 2024 declaratory ruling treats AI-generated voices as artificial voices under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act, which brings prior consent requirements to those calls. Callbacks to people who asked for one are a different matter, and your counsel can tell you how your state treats them.
