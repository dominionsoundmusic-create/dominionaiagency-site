An AI receptionist vs answering service decision usually starts with one problem: calls are coming in when nobody can pick up. Before choosing, it helps to know exactly what each option does on a call, because the three choices here (software, a call center, or a person at your front desk) are not interchangeable.

This guide describes each one on its own terms first, then puts them side by side, then covers how many small businesses mix them. If you mainly want prices, the AI receptionist cost guide lists what providers publish.

What does a receptionist do?

The Bureau of Labor Statistics describes receptionists as workers who perform administrative tasks such as answering phones, receiving visitors and providing general information about their organization to the public and customers. Its list of typical duties includes answering calls and forwarding them, scheduling and confirming appointments, greeting customers and visitors, and entering customer information into the organization's database.

O*NET, the Labor Department's occupation database, adds detail for receptionists and information clerks: operating the phone system to answer, screen or forward calls, take messages and schedule appointments, along with tasks such as receiving payments and recording receipts.

The phone is only part of the job. A receptionist notices a regular customer walking in, hands over a clipboard, settles a billing question face to face, and fills quiet time with filing and mail. That in-person, judgment-heavy work is the part no phone service, human or AI, replaces.

What a staffed answering service does

A staffed answering service is a company whose live agents answer calls for many businesses. You forward your line to them during set hours or when your own phone goes unanswered, and they greet callers with your business name, follow a script you provide, take messages and, depending on the plan, schedule appointments or transfer urgent calls.

The value is a human voice and human judgment at hours you could not staff yourself. The limits are that the agent is covering several businesses, knows yours only through the script, and is billed by the minute or by the call. Ruby, for example, publishes per-minute billing for its live receptionists, while other services charge per call; Ruby's own comparison page explains how the two models differ in practice.

What an AI receptionist does

An AI receptionist is software that answers your calls in a synthetic voice. HighLevel's help center, which documents the platform we build on, describes Voice AI agents that answer questions from a connected knowledge base, collect caller details, book appointments into a calendar, trigger follow-up automations and transfer callers when a person is needed.

It answers at any hour, including nights and holidays. It says the same thing the same way on every call, which is good when the answer is right and a problem when the answer is out of date. It only knows what it has been given, so the quality of an AI receptionist depends heavily on how it was set up and how often it is updated. Which one fits which kind of business

Hire a receptionist when

People walk in all day, the front desk handles payments or paperwork, and enough of the work needs a person on site to justify a salary. A medical office or a busy salon often fits here, with something else covering the phone after closing.

Use an answering service when

After-hours callers often need a human voice, calls tend to be emotional or delicate, or you want a person deciding when to wake you up. Volume is modest enough that per-minute or per-call billing stays reasonable.

Use an AI receptionist when

Most calls ask the same handful of things, you want appointments booked at night and on weekends, and you are often on a job or with a customer when the phone rings. A field service crew or a solo practice often fits here.

Industry pages go further, because a law firm's intake call and a restaurant's reservation call are different problems. See AI by industry for your trade.

Combining them: AI first, a person when it matters

You do not have to pick only one. A common pattern looks like this, with every rule set by you.

  1. The AI picks up. On every call, or only when your own phone rings out, or only after closing.
  2. It handles the routine. Hours, directions, services, booking an opening on your calendar, taking a detailed message.
  3. It hands off on your rules. A caller asks for a person, mentions an emergency or raises something on your do-not-handle list, and the call transfers to your cell, your front desk or an answering service.
  4. Nothing gets lost. A summary of each call goes to the person who needs it, so the callback starts where the caller left off.

An in-house receptionist plus an AI receptionist for lunch, overflow and nights is another common mix. The person keeps the front desk; the AI picks up when the person cannot.

Will AI replace customer service?

For the economy as a whole, the Bureau of Labor Statistics projects employment of customer service representatives to decline 5 percent from 2025 to 2035. It expects automation and self-service to take over more routine tasks, while some companies keep people for complex questions. Openings will still come up every year as workers leave the occupation.

At the scale of a small business, the more practical reading is about which calls go where. The repetitive questions are what AI handles most consistently. The caller who is angry, confused or facing a hard decision is still better served by a person, which is why every system we set up has a way to reach one.

The honest limits of an AI receptionist

The National Institute of Standards and Technology lists confabulation, the production of confidently stated but false content, among the risks of generative AI. On a phone call, that means an AI receptionist can sound sure while being wrong if it is asked something outside the information it was given. Careful setup narrows that risk; it does not remove it.

Reduce the risk by

  • Giving it only facts you have checked, and updating them when they change
  • Telling it which topics to decline and hand to a person
  • Reading call summaries for the first weeks and fixing weak answers
  • Calling it yourself with the hard questions before customers do
